# UCR Technical Annex and first balance of hours

*Working translation. The Italian text is the authoritative one.*

**Calibration draft 0.1, not adopted.** Code 3 (Art. 9) entrusts to a Technical Annex the method for calculating the reproduction cost of goods and services, measured in Reproduction Cost Units (UCR). That annex has never been written. This page proposes a first version of it and uses it straight away for the question on which the whole system depends: are fifteen hours a week enough?

**The answer, in brief.** Counted on need, the Basket costs between 7 and 10 hours a week of internal labour. The manufactured goods required, also counted on need (goods that last, are repaired and are shared), add 1.4 to 2.7 hours. In all, between 9 and 13 hours: below the limit, with a margin of 2–6 hours. It is a first count, full of declared assumptions. The most complete independent study instead measures today’s way of producing and arrives, for a realistic minimum, at about 27 hours. The difference between 13 and 27 is what the project promises to eliminate: it is not proven, and only an experiment can confirm it.

## 1. What the UCR is

One UCR corresponds to one hour of ordinary Base Contribution. Materials, energy and logistics are converted into hours (Code 3, Art. 9, para. 1). The UCR is not a price, is not attributed to persons and is not a means of exchange (Art. 9, paragraphs 2 and 7). It serves three purposes: comparing alternatives, making visible how much labour a choice costs, establishing which body decides (Art. 7).

## 2. The method

The Code lists seven points that the Annex shall govern (Art. 9, para. 4). The draft proposes a rule for each.

| Point | Proposed rule |
|---|---|
| a) Time | One hour of labour is worth one UCR, whoever performs it and whatever the task. It is counted to the quarter hour. Preparation, tidying up and specific mandatory training are included. The increases for unpleasant tasks (Code 1, Art. 10) are recognition in CdR and do not change the UCR. |
| b) Materials | A material produced by the Community is worth the UCR required to produce it. A recovered material is worth the hours of recovery. A material purchased outside is worth the hours actually worked to produce it, when they are known; otherwise its price multiplied by the hours-per-euro coefficient of the country in which it is produced (see below). A durable good is spread in equal shares over the years of its useful life. |
| c) Energy | Energy produced by the Community is worth the UCR of the plant and its maintenance, divided by the kilowatt-hours produced over its useful life. Purchased energy is worth price times coefficient. |
| d) Digital services | Hours of maintenance of the Network plus shares of the devices, divided among those who use the service. |
| e) Time | No discount rate and no interest: an hour is worth an hour, today and in ten years. The hours-per-euro coefficient is updated every year. A value is recalculated when the data change by more than a fifth. |
| f) Certification | The Accounting Council certifies the value on the basis of the STV or the SVS (Art. 9, para. 5) and publishes, for each datum, the source and an uncertainty class: measured, estimated, assumed. |
| g) Revision and challenge | Annual revision. Anyone may challenge a value by bringing a datum; the Council replies with a public statement of reasons. |

**Logistics.** Hours of transport, plus the share of the vehicle, plus the energy consumed.

**An hour is worth an hour, whoever works it and wherever.** For external purchases a way is needed to go from euros to hours. Price alone is not enough, because it reflects wages and not hours: a good produced where labour is poorly paid costs few euros and contains many hours. Converting it with the European average would mean exchanging one hour of one’s own labour for many hours of someone else’s labour. The draft therefore proposes three rules, in order.

1. **Actual hours.** If the hours worked to produce a good are known, those are used.
2. **Coefficient of the country of production.** If they are not known, the price is multiplied by the hours of labour per euro of the country in which the good is produced. If it is produced in several countries, each part of the price has its own coefficient.
3. **European average.** If it is not known where it is produced, the coefficient of the economy in which the Community is located is used, and the value is certified as “assumed”.

The coefficient is the ratio between the hours worked and the output of an economy. In the European Union, in 2024: 353.7 billion hours for 18,043 billion euros, that is **0.0196 hours per euro**: 51 euros of output contain on average one hour of labour. For other countries the draft derives it from output per inhabitant, assuming the same hours worked per inhabitant as in the European Union.

| Country of production | Hours per euro | One hour of labour every |
|---|---|---|
| European Union | 0.020 | 51 euros |
| China | 0.064 | 16 euros |
| Brazil | 0.082 | 12 euros |
| Indonesia | 0.17 | 5.8 euros |
| Vietnam | 0.18 | 5.6 euros |
| Côte d’Ivoire | 0.31 | 3.2 euros |
| India, Bangladesh | 0.33 | 3.1 euros |
| Ethiopia | 0.75 | 1.3 euros |
| Rwanda | 0.80 | 1.2 euros |

An example of actual hours: in Rwanda coffee growing requires about 251 working days per hectare and yields on average 1.69 kg of cherries per plant. With 2,500 plants per hectare, seven-hour working days, six kilos of cherries per kilo of green coffee and 1.19 kilos of green per kilo of roasted (assumption), one kilo of roasted coffee contains about 3 hours of plantation labour. Converted with the European average, the same kilo would contain 0.3.

**What makes up the cost: labour, means of production, transport.** The real cost of a good has three parts: the hours of those who produce it, the share of the means used to produce it (machines, materials, energy, converted into hours) and transport. A country’s coefficient does not measure distance: it estimates how many hours are needed there, with the means of production used there. The coffee contains three hours because it is picked by hand, with few means; with better tools it would contain fewer, wherever it was grown. Transport is counted separately and weighs little: in May 2026 a container from Shanghai to Genoa cost about 4,100 dollars, that is about 20 cents per kilo of goods with a twenty-tonne load: less than one hundredth of a UCR.

These are indicative conversions (Art. 9, para. 6), to be improved with supply-chain data.

**What the UCR does not measure.** It does not measure environmental impact, which is assessed by the STV in accordance with Code 2. It does not include domestic work carried out in the Care Nuclei. It does not include civic functions, which appear in the Labour Balance and not in the cost of individual goods.

## 3. Worked example: one kilo of bread

The values marked “example” serve to show the method: they are not measurements.

| Step | Data | UCR |
|---|---|---|
| One hectare of wheat | 45 hours of labour (Italian regional tables); 400 euros of purchases and machine shares (example) = 7.8 UCR; yield 2,930 kg | 0.018 per kg of wheat |
| Mill | 15 hours per tonne, 0.05 kWh and 2 cents of share per kg (example); 95 kg of flour from 100 kg of wheat | 0.035 per kg of flour |
| Flour in the bread | 0.74 kg of flour per kg of bread | 0.026 |
| Labour at the oven | 15 kg of bread per hour of labour (example) | 0.067 |
| Oven energy | 0.7 kWh (example); one kWh purchased at 0.25 euros is worth 0.0049 UCR | 0.003 |
| Salt, yeast, share of the oven | 5 cents (example) | 0.001 |
| **One kilo of bread** | | **0.097 UCR, about 6 minutes** |

Two thirds of the cost is labour at the oven. The field weighs less than a fifth. It is the same result as the balance below: in food production, processing and cooking cost more hours than growing.

## 4. First balance of hours

The count concerns a Community of 150 persons with the age structure of the European Union, at steady state. It follows the categories of the Labour Balance (Code 1, Art. 2, para. 7). All the numbers can be recalculated and modified with the program `strumenti/bilancio_ore.py`, published in the repository.

### 4.1 Hours available

| Item | Persons | Equivalent contributors | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 18: no mandatory CBO | 27 | 0 | Eurostat: 17.8% |
| Between 18 and 69 | 100 | 100 | Eurostat: 66.7% |
| Between 70 and 74: CBO reduced by half | 8 | 4 | Eurostat: 5.3%; Code 1, Art. 11 |
| From 75: exempt | 15 | 0 | Eurostat: 10.2%; Code 1, Art. 11 |
| Women who have recently given birth and parents of children under three | | −4 | Eurostat: 2.57% children under three; Code 1, Art. 11 |
| Severe disability, caregivers, illness | | −5 | Assumption: 5% |
| **Total** | **150** | **95** | 63% of the population |

95 contributors times 15 hours times 52 weeks: **about 73,900 hours a year**, that is 493 hours per inhabitant. The count provides for no weeks of break: with 48 weeks the hours available fall to 68,200.

The 24 hectares are close to the 20 estimated in the Basket draft with another method. Nuts alone occupy more than a third of the land and more than half of the field hours: 75 grams a day is a costly quantity.

A check: for the same 150 persons the European Union today employs about 14,100 hours across agriculture, the food industry and catering. The 10,960 hours of the count are of the same order.

### 4.3 Manufactured goods, counted on need

Machines, tools, panels, building materials, medicines, textiles, electronics: the Community does not produce them itself. Here they are counted like food, starting from what is needed and not from what is consumed today. For each good: how much it costs, how many years it lasts, how many persons share it. Euros become hours with the coefficient of section 2.

| Group | What it includes | Euros per person a year | Hours a year for 150 persons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Person and Nucleus | Clothing (4 kg a year), bed, furniture, linen and tableware, refrigerator and cooker per Nucleus, washing machine in a common laundry, telephone, Nucleus computer, AI Node, bicycle, hygiene | 654 | 1,920 |
| Health | Medicines, medical supplies, share of the Basin’s equipment | 400 | 1,180 |
| Housing | Materials for 15 m² of dwelling and 5 m² of common buildings per person, over 80 years | 188 | 550 |
| Energy and water | Photovoltaics (2 kW per person), storage, heat, water network, spare parts | 281 | 830 |
| Production | Agricultural machinery, mill, oil mill, collective kitchen, workshop, seeds and materials | 253 | 750 |
| Mobility and Network | Three light electric vehicles, 3,000 km a year of public transport, servers and equipment | 466 | 1,370 |
| Learning | Materials and instruments | 50 | 150 |
| **Total** | | **2,291** | **6,740** |

That is 1.4 hours a week per contributor. The inventory is converted with the European average. With the rule of section 2 a good made half in China contains about twice the hours: doubling the whole inventory gives 2.7 hours.

**What this inventory is worth.** Prices and lifetimes are drafting assumptions, listed one by one in the program: they are to be replaced with real quotations. The result, however, does not depend on a wrong price: even tripling the entire inventory the complete count stays below fifteen hours. For comparison, today’s European manufacturing employs about 14,200 hours for 150 persons, food industry excluded: the inventory on need asks for less than half of that.

### 4.4 The complete count

| | A: essential | B: four services at European level |
|---|---|---|
| Internal labour | 7.2 | 10.4 |
| Manufactured goods counted on need | 1.4 to 2.7 | 1.4 to 2.7 |
| **Total, hours a week per contributor** | **8.6 to 10.0** | **11.7 to 13.1** |
| Margin below fifteen hours | 5.0 to 6.4 | 1.9 to 3.3 |

In a Community that lives within an external economy, manufactured goods are paid for by the Common Fund (Code 15, Articles 17 and 19-bis), not with CBO hours: the count says how many hours would be needed if the Confederation produced them.

### 4.5 What the count does not include

1. **Housework in the Nuclei.** Today in Europe each person devotes on average about three hours a day to home and family, of which 30–50 minutes to preparing meals. The count brings into the CBO only one collective meal a day, the nursery, the laundry and the cleaning of common spaces: 1.7 hours a week per contributor. Everything else remains in the Nuclei and is not counted.
2. **Some items not estimated:** repair of tools, mandatory training, civic protection, time for passing on competences.
3. **Start-up.** Building the Community costs more than maintaining it.
4. **What the project eliminates.** Commerce, advertising, finance, insurance: they are not in the count, because in the system of Circles they do not exist. It is a choice of the project, not a datum.

### 4.6 Three external comparisons

| Comparison | Datum | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Today’s European economy | 787 hours of paid labour per inhabitant a year | The fifteen-hour limit makes available 493 hours per inhabitant: 63% of the paid labour that Europe uses today. |
| An independent study (McElroy and O’Neill, 2025, on the United Kingdom) | For indispensable goods alone: 17.5 hours a week. Adding what the authors consider necessary for the scenario to be realistic (government, security, universities, childcare, insurance, capital maintenance): 36.7. For a “good life” at the minimum levels asked for by residents: 45.5. Today’s British consumption: 64.7 | Brought to the basis of this count they are 12.7, 26.6, 32.9 and 46.9 hours of CBO a week: the conversion is ours. The study counts all sectors and the labour embodied in imports, with today’s way of producing; it does not count unpaid care work. |
| Real income-sharing communities (United States) | Twin Oaks, about 100 adults: between 36 and 45 hours a week depending on the source. East Wind, 75 persons: 35 hours. Acorn: 42 hours | The quotas are per adult member and include cooking, cleaning, childcare and the work for the market that finances purchases. They are small communities, little mechanised and with few elderly people: the comparison is indicative. |

**Where the difference between 13 and 27 hours comes from.** The count of this draft and the study measure two different things. The study measures the world as it is: in its minimum scenario 29% of the hours are agricultural labour, largely carried out where an hour of labour produces little, and 40–45% are services; the items added to make it realistic weigh more than all the rest. This count measures the system as the project designs it: agriculture with European yields and equipment, administration limited to one tenth of the hours, no commerce, no finance, no insurance, goods that last and are shared. The difference is therefore the project’s wager, put into numbers. The authors of the study reach a similar conclusion: reducing consumption is not enough, the way of producing has to change.

### 4.7 What can be drawn from it

1. **Counted on need, the count adds up.** Between 9 and 13 hours a week, everything included, against a limit of 15.
2. **The margin is 2–6 hours, and the uncertainty is of the same order.** Many items are assumptions. The count holds even tripling the manufactured goods, but it is not a measurement.
3. **The result holds for the system as it is designed, not for today’s way of producing.** With that, the independent estimate gives about 27 hours. The fifteen hours depend on what the project changes: no waste, no intermediation, durable goods, light administration. These are the things to verify first.
4. **Everything depends on where the boundary between CBO and Nucleus lies.** If all of today’s housework entered the CBO, it alone would be worth 33 hours a week per contributor. The texts leave to each Community the choice of what to make collective: it is the decision that weighs most on the limit.
5. **Health and education cannot be sustained with 150 persons.** Scenario A asks for 0.7 health workers and 1.9 teachers full-time by today’s standards: numbers too small to guarantee a service. They are to be shared within the Basin, as the Constitution provides for the hospital (Article 5.1).
6. **The limit applies to each person, not to the sum.** Specialist hours are divided among those who are qualified (Code 1, Art. 6, para. 3). At 780 hours a year each, health care at European level requires 9 qualified persons out of 95; the minimum level, between one and two. And agricultural work is seasonal: the nut harvest is concentrated in a few weeks, while the permitted peak is 25 hours for two weeks (Code 1, Art. 6, para. 5).

Code 1 already provides for what happens if the hours are not enough: deferrable activities are reduced, documentation is simplified, recourse is had to volunteering and to mutualism between Circles (Art. 2, para. 8). And it indicates the quarterly Labour Balances as the basis for verifying the limit (Art. 2, para. 9). This draft is the count made beforehand; those Balances will be the count made afterwards.

## 5. Robots and automation

The count in section 4 uses today’s machines. This section says how an automation is counted, where it is most needed and how much it can change the result. The rules in force are in Code 7, Article 6, which applies to every automation. Specific rules for robots are an open proposal ([proposal 8](https://imthezeroit.github.io/progetto-dei-cerchi/en/register/proposals.html)): where this section uses it, it says so.

### 5.1 How it is counted

A robot is a means of production, so it enters the count like any other: the hours required to build it, plus energy and maintenance, spread over the years in which it works. It is worthwhile when the hours it saves are more than those it costs. Code 7 already requires that the time saving be documentable (Art. 6, para. 1); proposal 8 asks for this comparison to be made before the machine is introduced.

An example: a machine costing 60,000 euros that lasts fifteen years costs 78 UCR a year, 157 if it is produced half where labour yields less. With fifty hours of maintenance that is between 130 and 210 hours a year. It is worthwhile if it saves more than that.

The saving does not go to whoever owns the machine, because no one owns it: the time freed remains with the Community, which decides how to use it (Code 7, Art. 6, para. 6). Proposal 8 confirms this rule for robots too.

### 5.2 Where it is needed, area by area

| Area | Hours a year in scenario A | What machines can do | What remains for persons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fields | 4,600 | Soil tillage, sowing, irrigation, mechanical weeding. Cereals, legumes and sunflower are already almost entirely mechanised. | The largest part of today’s labour: harvesting and tending nuts, vegetables and fruit. This is where robots would change the count most. |
| Food processing | 1,800 | Mill, oil mill, kneading, washing, packaging | Control, recipes, cleaning of the plants |
| Mines, excavation and raw materials | outside the internal count: it lies in the manufactured goods | Excavation, transport and crushing in hazardous environments. It is the first place from which to remove persons (Code 7, Art. 2, para. 6). | Design, supervision, maintenance, remediation. Reuse and recycling come before extraction: today no text permits new extraction (Const. 14.2). |
| Construction and maintenance | 4,430 | Excavation, handling of loads, prefabrication, inspections at height | Design, finishing, custom repairs |
| Logistics and Storehouse | 2,560 | Inventory, handling, part of the deliveries | Delivery to frail persons, relations with those who collect |
| Energy and water | 3,350 | Measurement, regulation, alarms: largely already the case | Repairs, decisions on priorities |
| Documentation | up to 3,560 | AI prepares minutes, registers and calculations | Every decision: AI proposes, it does not decide (Const. 19.6) |
| Collective kitchen | 4,560 | Better equipment | Cooking and serving, in the prudent assumption of this count |
| Care, health, learning, justice | 8,600 | Only tools operated by a person: hoists, aids, diagnostics | Everything else. The Code prohibits automating care, mediation, the validation of competences and guarantee decisions (Code 7, Art. 6, para. 2). |

**Raw materials.** A Community of 150 persons does not dig mines: metals and minerals come from outside, inside the manufactured goods. The Constitution prohibits any activity that causes irreversible damage to soil and water (Article 14.2): read literally, it prohibits any new mine. Two open proposals ([6 and 7](https://imthezeroit.github.io/progetto-dei-cerchi/en/register/proposals.html)) qualify the subsoil as a common good and ask each Community for an inventory of what can be recovered from buildings, vehicles and landfills. Whether and when to permit extraction remains an open question.

### 5.3 How much the count can change

In scenario A the labour that machines can lighten (fields, processing, energy and water, maintenance, logistics) is about 16,700 hours: just over half of the internal labour. Labour between persons (cooking, care, learning) is about 13,200 hours, and the project chooses not to automate it.

| If machines remove | Hours a week per contributor | Counting that machines cost one fifth of what they save (assumption) |
|---|---|---|
| nothing more than today | 7.2 | 7.2 |
| a quarter of those hours | 6.3 | 6.5 |
| half | 5.3 | 5.7 |
| three quarters | 4.4 | 5.0 |

Every ten per cent less is worth 23 minutes a week for each person.

### 5.4 Outlook

1. **There is a limit, and it is intended.** Even automating everything that can be automated, labour between persons remains: between three and four hours a week. The project does not aim at zero hours: it aims to free persons from dangerous and repetitive labour.
2. **Machines are not free.** They cost hours, energy and raw materials. The real limit is ecological before it is technical.
3. **Who gains is the difference from today.** Today a machine that replaces labour takes income away from someone and gives it to whoever owns it. Here it takes the Basket away from no one and reduces everyone’s hours.
4. **No promises.** The count of this draft holds with the machines that exist. Better robots would improve it; the project does not depend on them.

## 6. A consequence for the thresholds of Code 3

Article 7 of Code 3 distinguishes operations of low value (below 500 UCR), medium value (from 500 to 2,500) and high value. With the proposed coefficient, 500 UCR correspond to about 25,500 euros of purchases and to 0.7% of the annual hours of a Community of 150 persons; 2,500 UCR to about 128,000 euros and to 3.4%. For a small Community the low threshold could be too high: a purchase of 20,000 euros would be decided by a Competence Circle. [Proposal 11](https://imthezeroit.github.io/progetto-dei-cerchi/en/register/proposals.html) links the two thresholds to the size of the Community: 100 UCR plus one per person, and 500 plus five per person, with 500 and 2,500 as the maximum.

## How to correct it

Anyone who has better data can change the assumption in the program, redo the count and [open an issue](https://github.com/ImTheZeroIt/progetto-dei-cerchi/issues/new), or write to [zerocirclesproject@gmail.com](mailto:zerocirclesproject@gmail.com). The most useful data are measured ones: actual hours of collective kitchens, vegetable gardens, workshops, existing communities.
